The	Ferryman
A	single-player	mythological	deckbuilder	built	on	a	deliberately	broken	optimization	problem

	CSS481	Game	Development

	F2P
	
Document:
	Concept	and	mechanical	design	rationale
1.	Concept
You	are	Charon,	ferryman	of	the	dead.	Souls	crowd	the	shore.	Your	boat	has	limited	space.
You	choose	who	crosses,	and	who	waits.
The	player	occupies	a	role	normally	filled	by	the	system	rather	than	the	protagonist.	Charon	is	not	a	hero,	does	not
fight,	does	not	grow	stronger	in	any	conventional	sense,	and	cannot	complete	his	task,	since	the	dead	never	stop
arriving.	The	game	is	therefore	not	about	victory	but	about	the	character	of	the	choices	made	under	a	permanent
constraint.
The	myth	supplies	the	constraint	for	free.	In	the	common	tradition,	the	dead	pay	an	obol	for	passage,	and	those	who
cannot	pay	are	left	to	wander	the	near	shore.	That	single	detail	gives	the	game	its	central	tension	without	any	invented
fiction:	mercy	has	a	price,	and	someone	always	pays	it.
Why	this	myth
Perspective	inversion.
	The	player	is	a	non-human	functionary	of	the	underworld,	not	an	adventurer.	This	satisfies
the	design	goal	of	twisting	genre	convention	rather	than	reskinning	it.
Episodic	structure.
	One	crossing	is	one	self-contained	round.	Content	can	be	added	incrementally	across	the
semester	without	redesigning	the	core,	which	is	a	significant	scope	advantage.
Immediate	legibility.
	A	playtester	with	no	explanation	understands	"pick	who	gets	on	the	boat"	within	seconds.
This	matters	for	the	workshop	format,	where	visiting	teams	play	cold	and	briefly.
Low	component	count.
	The	paper	prototype	requires	only	cards,	tokens,	and	a	drawn	boat.
2.	Core	loop
One	crossing	consists	of	five	steps.	The	Game	Master	(a	team	member)	handles	all	draws,	resolution,	and	opponent
logic.

The	role	of	the	deck
Delivered	souls	leave	behind	a	card	that	enters	the	player's	deck	for	use	in	later	crossings.	These	are	not	abilities	in	the
usual	sense	but	residue:	what	the	poet	left,	what	the	soldier	left,	what	the	grieving	mother	left.	Consequently	the	deck
becomes	an	accumulated	history	of	the	player's	choices,	and	a	player	who	ferried	only	the	paying	dead	will	hold	a	deck
materially	different	from	one	who	carried	the	destitute.	The	intent	is	that	neither	deck	is	strictly	superior,	only
differently	capable.

3.	The	design	problem
The	loop	as	stated	is,	structurally,	the	
0/1	knapsack	problem
:	a	fixed	capacity,	a	set	of	items	each	carrying	a	weight
and	a	value,	and	the	task	of	selecting	the	subset	that	maximizes	value	without	exceeding	capacity.
This	is	simultaneously	the	concept's	greatest	strength	and	its	most	serious	threat.
Why	the	structure	helps
Players	intuit	it	without	instruction,	which	is	essential	for	a	short	playtest	slot.
It	produces	a	decision	on	the	very	first	turn,	with	no	ramp-up	or	tutorial.
It	is	trivially	implementable	in	both	paper	and	digital	form.
Why	the	structure	threatens	the	game
A	knapsack	instance	with	full	information,	independent	items,	a	single	value	axis,	and	no	cross-turn	consequences	is
solvable
.	Players	converge	on	a	greedy	heuristic	(approximately,	value	per	unit	of	capacity),	after	which	every	turn
becomes	arithmetic	rather	than	judgment.	The	game	stops	being	played	and	starts	being	computed.
Design	thesis.
	The	objective	is	not	to	avoid	the	knapsack.	It	is	to	retain	the	knapsack's	legibility	while	systematically
violating	the	assumptions	that	make	it	solvable.	Each	violation	is	referred	to	below	as	a	
lever
.
This	framing	is	also	worth	stating	explicitly	in	the	final	report.	Demonstrating	an	understanding	of	
why
	the	naive
version	fails,	and	then	addressing	it	deliberately,	is	a	stronger	showing	than	arriving	at	a	playable	game	by	trial	and
error.
4.	The	levers
Six	levers	were	identified.	Each	attacks	a	different	assumption	of	the	classical	knapsack	formulation.
Lever	1:	Hidden	or	uncertain	value	
DEFER
Assumption	broken:	values	are	known	at	selection	time.
Each	soul	shows	a	visible	face	(appearance,	capacity	cost,	whether	it	carries	an	obol)	and	conceals	a	truth	(the	card	it
yields),	revealed	only	on	arrival.	A	well-dressed	man	paying	his	fare	may	prove	to	be	a	tyrant	whose	residue	is
worthless;	a	silent	beggar	may	prove	to	be	Orpheus.
Effect:
	converts	optimization	into	inference	under	uncertainty.	Some	partial	signalling	is	required,	otherwise	selection
degrades	into	pure	chance.
Lever	2:	Non-additive	value	
ADOPT
Assumption	broken:	item	values	are	independent	and	additive.
Souls	interact.	A	mother	and	child	carried	together	yield	strong	residue;	separated,	both	are	diminished	and	the	one	left
behind	sours	faster.	Two	soldiers	from	opposing	sides	provoke	a	conflict	during	the	crossing.	A	poet	raises	the	value	of
every	other	passenger	by	restoring	their	story	to	them.
Effect:
	souls	can	no	longer	be	evaluated	individually.	The	boat	becomes	a	composition	problem	rather	than	a	summation,
which	is	the	single	highest-leverage	change	available	and	costs	nothing	beyond	additional	card	text.
Lever	3:	Incommensurable	currencies	
ADOPT
Assumption	broken:	value	is	a	single	scalar.
Two	resources	are	tracked	separately	and	cannot	be	exchanged	for	one	another:
Obols.
	Material	payment.	Buys	capacity,	supplies,	and	safety.
Standing.
	What	the	carried	dead	leave	behind.	Determines	the	strength	of	the	deck	and	the	ability	to	survive	rough
water.
A	third	axis	may	be	layered	on	top:	a	periodic	
quota
	demanded	by	Hades,	denominated	specifically	in	obols,	which	must
be	satisfied	regardless	of	the	player's	preferred	approach.
Effect:
	multi-objective	problems	have	no	single	optimum	by	definition,	only	a	chosen	stance.	This	is	also	where	the	moral
weight	becomes	mechanical	rather	than	decorative,	since	refusing	a	paying	soul	in	favour	of	a	penniless	one	is	a	genuine
sacrifice	rather	than	a	clever	play.
Lever	4:	Temporal	coupling	
ADOPT
Assumption	broken:	each	instance	is	independent.

Crossings	are	not	isolated	problems.	Delivered	souls	enter	the	deck	and	become	the	tools	available	next	crossing.
Refused	souls	remain	on	the	shore,	worsening	over	time,	so	a	refusal	defers	a	cost	rather	than	avoiding	it.	Certain	souls
return	in	later	crossings,	changed	by	the	wait.
Effect:
	the	local	optimum	and	the	global	optimum	diverge,	which	is	the	condition	under	which	interesting	play	emerges.	A
player	who	maximizes	every	individual	crossing	will	accumulate	a	shore	full	of	wraiths.
Lever	5:	Stochastic	resolution	
DEFER
Assumption	broken:	outcomes	are	deterministic.
The	river	is	an	active	participant.	After	loading,	an	event	is	drawn	that	interacts	with	the	specific	cargo:	a	storm
endangering	the	heaviest	passengers,	a	siren	song	that	the	regretful	resist,	still	water	that	rewards	carrying	someone	at
peace,	or	the	attention	of	Cerberus,	drawn	to	overloaded	boats.
Effect:
	the	optimal	load	depends	on	a	distribution	the	player	can	only	estimate,	rewarding	attention	to	the	composition	of	the
event	deck	over	repeated	plays	rather	than	rewarding	calculation.
Lever	6:	Shifting	capacity	
DEFER
Assumption	broken:	capacity	is	a	fixed	constant.
Capacity	varies	with	river	conditions,	or	with	the	accumulated	weight	of	the	player's	own	deck,	on	the	reasoning	that	a
ferryman	burdened	by	memory	rides	lower	in	the	water.
Effect:
	removes	the	stable	planning	target	that	players	would	otherwise	optimize	against.	Powerful,	but	risks	obscuring	the
constraint	that	makes	the	game	legible	in	the	first	place.
5.	Proposed	levers	for	the	paper	prototype
Levers	2,	3,	and	4	are	adopted	for	the	paper	prototype.	Levers	1,	5,	and	6	are	deferred	to	the	digital	build.
Rationale	for	the	selection
Criterion
Assessment
Implementation	cost
All	three	adopted	levers	are	expressible	as	card	text	and	two	tracked	resources.	No	new	physical
components	are	required	beyond	what	the	base	loop	already	needs.
Coverage
They	attack	solvability	from	three	independent	directions:	item	interdependence,	objective	multiplicity,	and
cross-round	consequence.	A	player	cannot	defeat	all	three	with	one	heuristic.
Thematic	load
Each	adopted	lever	carries	meaning.	Lever	2	makes	relationships	matter,	Lever	3	makes	mercy	costly,
Lever	4	makes	refusal	consequential.	The	deferred	levers	mainly	add	texture.
Testability
Deferred	levers	introduce	randomness	and	variable	constraints,	both	of	which	make	it	harder	to	isolate
whether	the	core	decision	is	working	during	early	playtests.

7.	Open	questions
1.	
Is	the	first	crossing	genuinely	interesting?
	If	the	opening	decision	is	obvious,	the	shore	row	is	either	too	small	or
the	souls	are	insufficiently	differentiated.
2.	
Does	the	deck	stay	meaningful,	or	does	it	become	clutter?
	Deckbuilders	normally	include	a	culling	mechanism.
Whether	Charon	should	be	able	to	forget	the	dead	he	has	carried	is	both	a	mechanical	and	a	thematic	question,	and
the	answer	is	not	obvious.
3.	
How	punishing	should	wraiths	be?
	Too	weak	and	refusal	is	free;	too	strong	and	the	game	becomes	a	maintenance
chore.
4.	
Should	the	quota	escalate?
	An	escalating	quota	produces	an	arc	and	an	eventual	failure	state,	which	may	or	may
not	suit	a	game	about	a	task	that	never	ends.
5.	
Is	there	a	win	condition	at	all?
	Options	include	surviving	a	fixed	number	of	crossings,	a	score	based	on	final
standing,	or	an	explicitly	unwinnable	structure	judged	on	the	character	of	the	run.	This	should	be	settled	before	the
workshop,	since	testers	need	to	know	what	they	are	aiming	at.

Note	on	sources.
	Greek	myth	survives	in	multiple	conflicting	versions	across	Homer,	Hesiod,	Ovid,	Apollodorus,	and	later	retellings.	Details	of	Charon's
practice,	including	the	obol	and	the	fate	of	the	unpaid	dead,	vary	between	accounts	and	should	be	verified	against	a	specific	source	before	being
presented	as	authoritative	in	the	final	report.	The	mechanical	design	does	not	depend	on	which	version	is	adopted.
